Some issues you want set for you. Others you want to design magazine pages by hand: the headline exactly there, the wedding portrait tilted just so, a thin rule under the date because that is how you picture it. [Editor in Chief](/editor-in-chief) is the part of Atlas for the second kind. You get a blank page and every tool beside it, as many pages as you like, a proof that turns like the finished magazine, and a PDF at the end. No account, no upload, no allowance to spend.

## Start with a page, not a form

Open [Editor in Chief](/editor-in-chief) and the first page is empty. Under it, Atlas offers four starting points: **Cover**, **Poster**, **Story** and **Photo grid**, plus **Atlas Studio layouts…** for the designed pages from the Studio. Choosing one is a head start, nothing more. Every box it lays down is an ordinary box once it is on the page, so you can move, restyle or delete all of it.

Or skip the starters and build from the toolbar along the top:

1. **Heading** and **Paragraph** add text boxes. Double-click one to type in it.
2. **Photo** opens your files. You can also drop photographs straight onto the page; each lands where you let go of it, at a sensible size for its shape. Drop one onto an existing photo box and it fills that box instead.
3. **Frame** adds an empty picture box, for when you know the shape before you know the photo. Double-click it to choose one.
4. The three small buttons after it add a **Rectangle**, a **Circle** and a **Line**, for panels, bands of colour and rules.

Click any box to select it, drag to move it, pull its corners to resize. Edges snap to the page and to other boxes as you drag, with guides to show you what lined up; hold Alt while moving to turn snapping off.

## The panel beside the page

Everything about the selected box lives in the panel on the right. For words there is the **Type** section: a font, its size and weight, **Italic**, **Underline**, **Capitals**, **Letter spacing** and **Line height**, alignment across and down, and a colour. The list holds 24 faces, from Instrument Serif and Garamond to Futura, a typewriter and a handwriting face. They are fonts your computer already has, with sensible stand-ins on Windows, Mac and Linux, so nothing has to load before you can use them.

For a photograph, the panel offers **Zoom** and two focus sliders, **Focus left – right** and **Focus top – bottom**, to choose which part of the picture fills the frame. You can also double-click the photo and drag it about inside its frame; press Esc or double-click again when you are done. Then there is **Corners**, a **Border**, a **Shadow**, **Opacity**, **Rotate**, **Flip horizontally**, and a **Filter**: Original, Black & white, Sepia, Warm, Cool, Faded or Punchy. The filters are deliberately the kind a browser can draw exactly, so the PDF shows the same colour you chose on screen.

**Layer** brings a box forward or sends it back, which is how a headline sits over a photograph. **Align to page** centres or edges a box in one press. And **Undo** and **Redo** remember every step, with the keys you would expect: ⌘Z, ⇧⌘Z, ⌘D to duplicate, Delete to remove, arrows to nudge (Shift and an arrow nudges by ten).

## A magazine, not a single sheet

The difference between Editor in Chief and the [one-page poster](/poster) is the strip of pages under the canvas. **Add page** puts a blank one after the page you are on. **From Atlas Studio** adds a designed page from the Studio. On the page in hand you can **Switch layout**, **Move page earlier**, **Move page later**, **Duplicate page** or **Delete page**.

Studio pages bring one more convenience. When a page came from one of the Studio's themes, the strip offers **Next page**, which is that theme's next layout, so an issue can grow in one consistent style. Page numbers the layout printed keep themselves in step: add, move or delete a page and they renumber to match where each page now sits. Type over a page number and it becomes yours, and Atlas leaves it alone after that. If you would rather fill a Studio design as it is, with no rearranging, [the Studio itself](/studio) does that, and [our earlier spotlight](/blog/magazine-layout-with-your-own-photos) walks through it.

## See it bound before you print it

Press **Preview** and the whole issue opens as **Your issue, bound**: the cover stands alone, and every page after it faces its neighbour as it will once printed and folded. It is the same book Atlas uses to open a saved magazine, and each page in it is drawn by the same code that draws the editor and the PDF, so the preview cannot show you a page the file would not. This is where you notice that two photo-heavy pages face each other, or that the closing page sits on the left. **Download PDF** is right there when it looks right; **Back to editing** when it does not.

## Why it is built this way

**Your photographs stay in this tab.** A photo you add is read from your own disk and held in the page for as long as it is open. Nothing is uploaded to make your pages or your file.

**The file matches the screen.** The PDF and the picture are drawn in your browser by Atlas's own press, not by the print dialog, which on a phone would pick its own paper and margins. Pages are the magazine's size, 3:4, and come out at about 390 × 520 points, the same sheet the rest of Atlas prints on. The press is a large piece of code, so it is only fetched the first time you download.

**Nothing to sign up for.** Downloading the PDF, or the page you are on as a **PNG**, needs no account and does not count against any plan.

## Who it is for

Anyone who already sees the page in their head. A sister making the programme for a wedding's haldi, sangeet and pheras, one function to a spread. A parent putting together a first-birthday keepsake with the month-by-month photos in a grid and a large number on the cover. A class teacher setting a few pages for the school annual day, where every house's photograph needs its own place. A family's Diwali, one page per evening. Or the pages from a trip, with the boarding pass photographed and laid in at an angle.

If you would rather have the pages set for you from your photos and story, [the desk](/create) does that, pouring the words through the layouts and paginating them by measuring the type. Editor in Chief is the opposite: nothing is poured or fitted. Long text in a box simply runs as far as the box goes, so size your boxes for your words.

## What to know

- **Download before you close the tab.** Editor in Chief keeps your pages in the open tab only. There is no draft and no Save & share here, so a closed tab is a lost layout. Download the PDF as you go.
- **The PNG is one page**, the one you are on. The PDF is every page.
- **Fonts are your computer's.** The faces are system fonts with fallbacks, so a page can look a touch different on another machine, but the PDF you download is fixed once drawn.
- **Submit layout** sends the design of your pages to the [layout directory](/layouts), where other readers can start from it. The design is kept without your photographs, which come back as empty frames, and with every word replaced by placeholder text. The only picture kept is the one sample of the page you choose to attach and agree to show. Submitting needs a sign-in; designing and downloading do not.

## Try it this week

Pick one occasion from the last month, a birthday dinner or a weekend away, and give it four pages: a cover, a page of words, a photo grid and a closing photograph. Start the cover from **Cover**, the third page from **Photo grid**, and press **Preview** before you download. Four pages make two spreads and a cover, which is enough to see whether you like designing by hand, and small enough to finish in an evening.
